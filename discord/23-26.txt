[11/18/2021 4:31 PM] tweqx
Basically, there are two kind of scaling image algorithm in computer graphics. They differ when scaling an image with a non-power of two factor. Let's consider an example image, composed of 1 red pixel, and 1 blue pixel that create a 2x1 image. How would you resize this image to produce a 3*1 image ? 

The two kinds of algorithms are :
 1) Algorithms where pixels are mixed together on the grid to recreate the initial image with a different size. In our example, this would result in an image where the left pixel is red, the middle pixel is purple, and the right pixel is blue. The resulting image thus seems blurry. [where the exact implementations differ is regarding how to do the interpolation, using which maths formula]
 2) Algorithms were pixels are non-uniformly duplicated, to create the output image. In our example, the output image would be composed of two red pixel, and a blue pixel. As you can see, no pixels were "mixed", but a non-uniform duplication of pixels occurred. This result in a non-blurry but locally stretched image

With the hypothesis of the 57.jpg mayfly being an upscaled version of first mayfly, 3301 had to have to use a image scaling algorithm. We can clearly see that the 57.jpg mayfly isn't blurry at all, so the former kind of algorithm must have been used. However, as previously stated, this algorithm leaves the characteristic property that some areas of the scaled image are locally bigger than others. And what do we find in the 57.jpg mayfly ? "Block" that are 1 pixel larger than other blocks ! In my opinion, this observation is a strong argument towards the hypothesis that the 57.jpg was initially a small image (what 7he5haman calls the "reduced mayfly") enlarged.

Furthermore, the reduced mayfly overlaps perfectly the size of the original image of the mayfly that we found.

[3/30/2022 7:20 AM] some_porcupine
Consider an even more extreme example of a creature that has an apparently short lifespan: the mayfly.  Mayflies are notorious for their truncated lives, some apparently living for no more than 30 minutes.  In Germany, the mayfly is known as Eintagsfliege, or one-day fly.  In France, it is called the “ephemere”: the ephemeral, the transient, the fleeting.
(...)
Mayfly nymphs, or naiads (named after the beautiful young women of the water in Greek mythology) are autonomous, aquatic creatures, complete with 6legs and a full set of gills.  They feed on algae & other plant material, and they can move quickly to escape a predator by swimming in an up-and-down motion like a mini. dolphin.  MFs will stay in this naiad state – fully fx except for the ability to mate – for a year or more before they pupate and transform into the adult mayfly that we typically see swarming and then dying within a day.  A year is not a bad life span for an insect – at the very least, it’s a lot better than the single day we usually attribute to mayflies.  What’s more, the adult mayfly is not much more than a flying set of reproductive organs.  It doesn’t even eat – actually, it cannot eat even if it tried, because it has nonfunctional mouthparts and its gut is filled with nothing but air.  On the other hand, as a naiad, it leads a very active life: swimming, crawling, eating.  At the ripe old bug-age of a year or two, it molts, mates, and dies.

The adult dog-day cicada, like the mf, spends most of its life in its nymph stage, except the cicada nymph lives underground, contentedly nibbling on roots for 3 years.  The periodical ccd live even longer underground: 13 or 17 years.  When an adult ccd emerges, like the mf, it does not have much in terms of defense, which is quite unfortunate for a rather large, bulky, relatively slow-moving bug.   (...) But many cicadas do survive, because – like the mayflies – their sheer numbers are just too much for the predators to completely consume.
